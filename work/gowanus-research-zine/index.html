Project overview

The city as an accumulation of decisions.

Historical research, spatial mapping, and editorial storytelling offer three ways to read the city. Together, these studies examine how infrastructure, water, public resources, and redevelopment shape everyday urban life.

The group research traces a Brooklyn transect from Gowanus to Barclays Center. The individual mapping study examines access to knowledge and cultural institutions across New York. The Gowanus Gazette brings these questions into a newspaper-style publication about water, environmental health, and the changing waterfront.

01 / A landscape of continual intervention

Group research · Three weeks · June 2026

Across two centuries of change, each generation’s response to urban challenges creates the conditions for the next transformation. The transect reads Brooklyn through overlapping environmental, residential, civic, and infrastructural interventions.

Rather than treating urban history as a sequence of replacement, the project understands it as accumulation. Earlier structures and decisions remain present in the city’s form, its social relationships, and the pressures surrounding land and public space.

02 / Access to knowledge. Access to culture.

Individual study · DETQ / GIS & Rhino · One week · June 2026

This mapping study compares NYCHA public housing developments with libraries, museums, theaters, schools, and the subway network. A 500-meter radius is used to examine proximity to public resources, alongside a closer look at the distribution of residents under 18.

The study finds a contrast between the relatively broad distribution of libraries and the concentration of arts institutions in Midtown and Lower Manhattan. It asks how the geography of cultural infrastructure and transit shapes the opportunities available to public housing communities.

03 / The Gowanus Gazette

Group publication · Two weeks · June–July 2026

The city runs on water.
But who controls it?

A newspaper-style zine uses Gowanus to reveal water as an urban system connecting infrastructure, ecology, public health, and daily life. Historical research and data visualizations sit alongside news, comics, and satire.

Following a raindrop makes the combined sewer system legible. Stories of waterfront living, contamination, flood exposure, and redevelopment connect hidden infrastructure to visible urban change. The final pages consider alternative futures and ways to participate in the watershed’s care.
